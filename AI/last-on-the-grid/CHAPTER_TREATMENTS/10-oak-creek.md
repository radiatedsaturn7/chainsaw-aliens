# Mission 10 — Still Here
**Oak Creek Canyon, Arizona · PROPOSED; central character confrontation**

## Narrative question
Rob can see Cait caring for him when he offers nothing useful. Why does he still need the words? And why does Cait find those words harder to say when he demands them?

## Beginning
The difficult Utah leg leaves Rob exhausted or temporarily unable to perform precise repairs. He is still a skilled mechanic; forcing himself to work would make things worse. Cait takes over, handles the car competently, and gets a small pleasure for him—coffee, a cassette, something he likes—from farther away than she admits. She wants to spend a quiet evening together. Rob wants to find a task that justifies his presence.

Their conversation begins ordinarily. She complains about weather, a sensor or a local road. He notices how much she has been doing. **He is not oblivious to her affection.** He can list what she has done and his frustration is precisely that she refuses to call it love.

## The argument
Cait insists she isn't staying because she needs a mechanic. The draft should build toward the user's strong anchor line, keeping its force:

> CAIT: “You think I don't know how to fix a car? I'm an android, Rob. I've got the entire history of humanity on a little chip in my head. You really think that's why I've been keeping you around?!”

Rob may say something like, “Then why the fuck won't you say you love me?”—but give the exchange time to breathe before and after. He is not simply interrogating her with a sequence of five-word questions. He can speak in full, angry, halting thoughts. Cait can explain that she refuses to promise an inner experience she cannot verify, especially since her own original design may have shaped it. She is hurt that he keeps treating acts of care as insufficient, but she must not be forced to say the words. His wanting them is valid; pressure is not.

The argument **does not end with a TED talk or a neat hug**. Cait needs to scout a canyon bypass. They part irritated and fail to establish an exact return time.

## Playing it
The player drives as Cait, searching for a road around a landslide or blocked pass. Intermittent canyon radio makes communication unreliable. The player knows Cait is doing something practical, not deserting Rob. This creates **dramatic irony**: the player witnesses his fear when they reunite and understands it wasn't warranted by Cait's actions.

## Closing — the return
After an unnerving delay, Cait reappears. Rob has convinced himself she's gone. She has an entirely legitimate grievance: she told him she was checking the road, and he didn't listen. He can admit, without an explanatory soliloquy, that he thought she left. She is angry; she says she's allowed to be angry without abandoning him. Then she goes to get the map, or asks whether he's coming to inspect the alternative route. There is a trace of normal banter, but **not resolution**.

## What each wants
Rob wants to know he matters in a way that can be spoken. Cait wants him to understand that she is there because she *chooses* to be and that her silence on one word is not a denial of everything else. Both want the trip to continue; neither is certain what continuing means.

## Consequence / next chapter
They still need to reach the Pacific, with no magical settlement of their argument. The last leg should feel changed because of what they now know about each other. They can have quieter space together without resolving the word “love.”

## Second reading
Earlier references to repairs and invitations were never merely practical. Cait has repeatedly asked Rob along; he has repeatedly assumed she needs him. Her return is neither proof that she can never leave nor an oath to stay forever.

## Drafting caution
This is **not** a story of Cait becoming Rob's therapist. Don't make his mistrust her responsibility to cure. Do not let the player lose control of the scene's emotional meaning through a contrived illness or abrupt disabling of Rob. The canyon detour must remain a satisfying racing challenge.
