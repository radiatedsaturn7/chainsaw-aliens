# Author's Companion — Discussion Questions and Review Prompts
**v0.1 · For writers, editors and hypothetical readers; not a compulsory answer key**

## Before reviewing a chapter
- What does each character want **from this encounter**, apart from the subject the author wants to explore?
- Whose point of view are we in during the drive, and what does the player know that one character does not?
- Is there a scene action or mechanical consequence we could use instead of a line of explanation?
- Is the ending changed by the chapter, or could this scene be removed without anyone noticing?
- Where is the joy, irritability, memory, weather, or mundane nonsense that makes these people feel lived-in?

## Chapter prompts
**01 Tower Hill:** Is “Come back while you still can” a request, a warning or a disguised confession? What evidence could support different answers? Why is the Atlantic the “wrong ocean” to Cait?

**02 Mount Washington:** Does Cait want to confirm the absence of survivors, to understand Rob, or simply to go somewhere difficult? Is it possible for all three motives to be true?

**03 Laurel Highlands (optional):** Why is Rob willing to feed a dog when their supplies are limited? Does the act require anything of the dog? Would cutting this chapter weaken the theme or merely improve pacing?

**04 Ohio River:** Why does Rob keep calling when he should recognize an automated answer? Is Cait obligated to ask him about it? How does silence affect the player more effectively than a speech?

**05 Machine City:** Is repeated labor without customers necessarily meaningless? Can working robots form new purposes without being humanlike? Does the scene set up the scientist encounter without simply announcing the connection?

**06 Heaven:** Who, if anyone, abandoned whom? How does a mass departure over only a few years compare with a Rapture, and is Rob's outside life a purgatory or a deliberate choice? Does perfection eliminate some forms of living while making others possible? Does Cait's insistence on physical rain reveal preference, fear or stubbornness? Could Evelyn be right about her own happiness?

**07 The Other Scientist:** What do the android's easy loving words prove? What does Cait's disobedience prove—and what does it leave unknowable? How do we avoid reducing the other android to a shocking prop?

**08 Pikes Peak:** Is Cait enjoying the race a threat to Rob, or is Rob making it one? Does the audience genuinely want her to have the option of staying? What makes this chapter joyful?

**09 Moki Dugway:** Can Cait be morally right to help and still wrong to decide unilaterally? Does Rob object to the actual danger or to the loss of his role? What is left unresolved after the confession?

**10 Oak Creek:** Why does Rob need verbal love even when he can list Cait's caring actions? Does Cait's silence protect honesty or avoid vulnerability? Is she allowed to be angry and still care? Does the player see that the route-scouting absence is not abandonment?

**11 California:** What meaning does Cait's destination have apart from Rob? What is different about the first and last times we see the map? Can arrival feel complete without deciding the reserved final fate?

## Two readings, not one answer
Ask a reader to defend *both* of the following with actual scenes:
- “Rob fears abandonment because of what others did to him” **and** “Rob sometimes creates distance by demanding guarantees nobody can give.”
- “Cait's refusal protects her honesty” **and** “Cait sometimes fails to understand what her words mean to Rob.”
- “Heaven's residents left the world behind” **and** “Rob chose not to enter the life they offered.”
- “Rob taking Cait west is generous” **and** “Rob may be frightened of reaching the end.”

If the second reading has no evidence, the ambiguity hasn't been earned.

## Editorial scorecard (not a grade for literature)
Mark each criterion **SUPPORTED / WEAK / ABSENT** and cite the draft:
1. A concrete situation, independent of the symbolism.
2. Distinct wishes or fears for each participant.
3. Dialogue that can ramble and change rhythm without losing tension.
4. A real consequence that carries into the next chapter.
5. At least one apparently ordinary detail with possible second meaning.
6. A plausible opposing interpretation or point of view.
7. No unwanted reintroduction of a rejected or reserved plot point.
8. Racing that would be enjoyable even if the player missed all subtext.

## What the guide is *not*
An English teacher can discover new interpretations without those interpretations having been consciously planted. This guide is for finding weak craft and supporting multiple readings, **not for guaranteeing Salinger-level greatness by filling in enough symbolism**. If the best parts are funny, odd and hard to summarize, protect them from the checklist.
