# The Things We Leave Behind — Literary Companion and Teacher's Guide
**Companion to [Chapter One screenplay](../SCRIPTS/01-rhode-island-full-draft.md) · Draft 1 · 2026-11-24 · Author/editor-facing only**

This document is a critical reading **of evidence on the page**, not a list of conclusions the reader must believe. It serves four uses: (1) a literary guide for the long-form story, (2) cutscene direction and writing notes, (3) design/QA tests for the playable chapter, and (4) preservation of second-playthrough meanings that must be supported without prematurely revealing them.

**A warning about certainty:** A motif is not a fact. We know what Rob and Cait do and say in the draft. We often do **not** know what they secretly mean, whether one of them correctly understands another, or what the machine civilization experiences.

---

## Chapter's spine

**Literal plot:** Cait comes home with fuel in a blizzard. Her long-planned Pacific journey is interrupted when she insists on seeing why the machines are dismantling recently rebuilt Boston. At Blue Hills, a tower collapses; Rob realizes their Providence lab may be threatened. They race back and find it systematically dismantled. Cait and Rob salvage what they can. They leave by RI 116, where a road brings Rob face to face with the memory of a former lover. On Putnam Pike, indifferent construction machines force them off the road. They escape through Pachaug State Forest and camp just beyond Rhode Island. Cait wants a White Mountains detour next. Rob complains, but remains with her.

**Immediate dramatic question:** Will they get out of home territory intact, and what will losing the lab do to Rob?

**Long-range question introduced:** Why are the machines building, demolishing and rebuilding civilization, apparently without concern for human-scale life?

**Relationship question:** When Cait freely chooses a destination, does Rob accompany her because he promised, because she needs his help, or because he wants to be with her? The chapter offers several clues, not an answer.

### The chain of consequence

1. Cait chooses to look at Boston → they climb Blue Hills.
2. Dismantling in Boston prompts fear for Providence → they race home.
3. They find the laboratory gone → the trip no longer has a safe return point.
4. Rob's road-avoidance reveals a painful memory → the journey becomes personal, not merely geographic.
5. Construction activity on RI 44 displaces them again → they flee to Pachaug.
6. Surviving the forest opens room for Cait's next ordinary choice → the New Hampshire detour.

Notice that two of these causes are **Cait's preferences**, not a robot plot. To make the entire chapter a relentless machine attack would erase her freedom. To make it six unrelated races would erase the forward drive.

## Close reading — scene by scene

### 1. Tower Hill: “Come back while you still can”

**Textual evidence:** Rob's warning is fragmented over radio. Cait continues controlling the car and answers when it is safe. Back at the garage, Rob stops his soldering at “We still heading out tomorrow?” and answers “That's what I promised.”

**First reading:** A familiar domestic scene after a dangerous run. One character worries; the other can handle herself. The promise sounds like a practical commitment.

**Second reading:** “Come back” can be heard as a fear of being left alone again. “That's what I promised” may be Rob's way of avoiding the more vulnerable answer: “I want to go with you.”

**Counterreading:** He may simply be preoccupied, tired, or genuinely worried about weather and supplies. His pause does not prove possessiveness, and Cait is not obligated to interpret it that way.

**Performance note:** The garage must not feel like a memorial tableau. Let tools click and the heater sputter. The two are used to one another's rhythms.

**Failure test:** If a voiceover announces that Rob fears abandonment, the scene stops working.

### 2. The map and the M3: the difference between choosing and maintaining

**Textual evidence:** Cait marks and revises routes on paper; Rob argues about packing tools and tires. She says he doesn't have to come. He says the tires need changing. The M3 is broken but clearly retained; he deflects when asked about it.

**Possible reading:** Cait is already living a life that includes preferences she cannot justify as tasks. Rob expresses affection as preparation, practical competence and accompaniment. The stored car suggests something he refuses to discard even though it has no present utility.

**Counterreading:** His tire concerns are realistic and important in a racing game. A reasonable reader can simply take him for an experienced mechanic. Not every tool symbolizes fear.

**Second-playthrough seed:** The M3 is Jim's. It offers a concrete emotional object before the player learns the backstory. Nothing about the first encounter requires the player to guess the owner.

**Failure test:** No explicit “brother,” death explanation, or flashback in Chapter 1. The reader should remember an oddly protected car, not decode an obvious memorial.

### 3. Blue Hills: the destruction of something recently built

**Textual evidence:** Boston has been reconstructed by autonomous machines and is now being systematically dismantled. Rob's first response is irritation; the falling tower changes him. Cait starts “You don't think—”; he demands that they leave.

**First reading:** Rob recognizes danger to their home before Cait finishes the thought. An apparently large-scale construction mystery becomes a reason to hurry.

**Possible deeper reading:** The machine civilization can assemble and remove physical structures without sharing any human idea of attachment. A tower may be a tool, experiment, expression or something beyond the characters' categories.

**Counterreading:** The machines might have perfectly practical reasons to dismantle a building. The chapter supplies no evidence that their actions are irrational, cruel, lonely, or self-destructive.

**Cinematic direction:** Stage the collapse clearly enough that the player understands a *real, consequential change* has occurred. Avoid a hostile machine looking straight at the camera or a musical sting implying a villain has noticed the protagonists.

**Failure test:** Neither character says “hive mind,” “superintelligence,” “consciousness,” or “Heaven's brain.” These are private hypotheses for later authoring.

### 4. Providence: “Again”

**Textual evidence:** A familiar workshop has been removed with technical precision. Rob notices a ratchet, a threshold, the heater, the hook arrangement. He says he will have to rebuild everything “Again.” Cait reminds him they were already leaving. He knows, but cannot move on immediately.

**First reading:** Rob's laboratory has been demolished before. A literal repairman is confronting yet another rebuild.

**Second reading (future, if earned):** “Again” can include a different earlier loss of home, not solely repeated demolition by machines. He may feel not only that his property is ruined but that *home can be revoked*. This is an undercurrent, not an explanatory answer delivered by the player-facing chapter.

**Counterreading:** The reaction is justified even without a hidden biography. Losing a workshop is a practical catastrophe: tools, comfort, accumulated work and safety are gone. Don't imply grief is meaningful only because it stands for an earlier wound.

**Acting note:** Let Rob talk about the bench and the screwdriver rather than grandly proclaim loss. His attention to tiny recoverable objects is behavior a performer can play.

**Double echo:** Boston and the lab share a physical cause but not a scale of meaning. A collapsing tower is spectacular; the broken threshold matters more to Rob.

**Failure test:** No exposition about a specific childhood foreclosure, family circumstance, or real biography is necessary in this chapter. If a later scene reveals an earlier home loss, the earlier “Again” should remain plausible without it.

### 5. The house: memory without an answer

**Textual evidence:** Rob initially wants to avoid RI 116. When Cait offers him an alternate road, he chooses to go anyway. Outside the old house, he tells a ridiculous flashlight story, recalls an illuminated window, says “She went to Heaven,” and wonders if she thinks of him. Cait does not answer on the absent woman's behalf.

**First reading:** A man revisits a place connected with a deceased former lover. “Heaven” is heard in its familiar afterlife sense. His grief may be complicated by a relationship that ended before death.

**Second reading after Data Center City:** Heaven is a digital existence into which people voluntarily entered. The phrase can mean she left the physical world and perhaps chose a life without him. The script **must not** seed any hint that he could call, visit, or recover her before the reveal.

**Counterreading:** One cannot infer that he was abandoned, that she was unkind, that they had a tragic last conversation, or that she still remembers him. Rob admits only that he loved her and thinks of her sometimes.

**Important relationship move:** Cait initially pushes for her road, but then offers him another. This matters more than a triumphant declaration that “my trip means my rules.” She wants freedom, not the pleasure of disregarding his boundaries. Rob's choice to take the painful road is his own.

**The joke's function:** The sabotaged-tire story reveals a warm, foolish past self; the final Pulitzer remark releases tension without erasing it. Humor is not garnish on a lesson. Humor is how these two can be near the subject without naming it.

**Later callback, not written here:** At Heaven, Cait might ask whether there is anyone else Rob wants to see. A quiet refusal can echo the house without explaining whether the former lover remembers him. The callback must occur **after** the audience learns what Heaven is.

**Failure test:** Do not put “she went to Heaven” in a codex entry that explains uploading before Chapter 6.

### 6. Putnam Pike: being irrelevant is dangerous

**Textual evidence:** The convoy alters the roadway while the car is on it; Rob says “We're in their way!” It does not pursue them or acknowledge the wreck. Cait asks whether they were deliberately run off.

**Reading:** The machine world's apparent indifference is more disturbing than routine hostility. The protagonists are physically vulnerable despite their ingenuity.

**Counterreading:** Indifference to a car does not prove that the system is unconscious, unintelligent, malicious or incapable of knowing what it does. An observer can be unable to infer motives from behavior.

**Player-facing concern:** A race cannot feel unfairly lost to an unavoidable scripted obstacle. Either author a readable dodge with strong warnings or use a controlled cutscene after a winnable section. The race must demonstrate skill, not negate it.

**Failure test:** No enemy bark, target lock, chase music keyed to the player's identity, or on-screen “ROBOTS ATTACK” label. This is construction, not a personal assassination.

### 7. Pachaug: a return to small things

**Textual evidence:** The threatening machines remain behind, the forest is quiet, Rob gets stuck in mud, Cait mocks him, and they cooperate. Cait worries that choosing to leave cost him his home. He does not let her carry the whole blame. A future detour is proposed before the chapter ends.

**Reading:** The physical journey continues despite incomplete emotional answers. Ordinary mishaps let the relationship feel worth following. New Hampshire is a *want*, not a clue chained to the machines.

**Counterreading:** Rob's acceptance doesn't mean the loss is resolved. Cait's thoughtfulness doesn't cancel her stubbornness. The trip remains a choice both can reconsider.

**Handoff requirement:** Chapter 2 should open on Hurricane Mountain Road with Rob's “Why the hell are we here again?” and Cait's Kancamagus explanation; the conclusion of 2-1 should reverse their positions, with **Rob** wanting another scenic road and enjoying the experience.

## Symbols, motifs, and their limits

| Recurring object or action | Plausible significance | What it must *not* be reduced to |
|---|---|---|
| The map | Cait's chosen direction; the ability to revise a plan | A compass labeled FREE WILL |
| A lit room, then its absence | A place that once welcomed someone home | Proof every house means the same thing |
| Building / dismantling | One pattern in machines and Rob's habits | Confirmation the machines are depressed |
| The M3 | A specific car retained despite no immediate usefulness | A quest item whose value equals the reward |
| “Again” | Repetition of physical and emotional loss | A required lore puzzle with one answer |
| “Heaven” | Death on first read; a different kind of departure later | Permission to reveal uploads early |
| Tools, tires, jack, heater | Competence and ordinary life, sometimes tenderness | Symbolism every time an object appears |
| West / wrong direction | Cait sets the destination and can make detours | An excuse for random geography |

**One subtle pair worth preserving:** The machines disassemble Boston and Rob's lab; Rob salvages a car and a screwdriver. The chapter never declares one behavior meaningless and the other meaningful. It lets scale and attachment do the work.

## Structural and adaptation critique

**What the prose may do:** Show a viewpoint, emotional pacing, sensory detail, ambient physical world, and transitions.

**What the player must actually receive:** A visible Boston collapse, urgent return, ruined lab, “Again,” optional M3 recognition, the former lover's house and “She went to Heaven,” a machine-induced road hazard, a Pachaug escape, and a New Hampshire choice.

**What should not be forced into a UI:** Internal interpretations, the teacher's guide, an omniscient claim about the machine intelligence, or unearned flashbacks.

### Pacing target, not a fixed timer

- 1-1: Immediate drive, brief garage introduction. Keep the current fail-forward cold open.
- 1-2: A real playable climb before the destruction reveal.
- 1-3: Urgent drive home; allow silence before the lab reaction.
- 1-4: Unthreatening driving, slower dialogue afterward. This emotional contrast is essential.
- 1-5: A readable change from road run to obstacle avoidance.
- 1-6: Dirt/gravel escape; close with breathing room and a specific invitation to the next region.

The screenplay proposes **Providence as 1-3**; the current game data still places Providence as the stage-one regional boss after Pachaug. That is a real implementation conflict. It is not solved by writing a new sequence number on paper. Build work would need to change the path and boss gating, or stage the Providence narrative as a non-boss revisiting of the existing course.

### Player-agency audit

- Cait decides to visit Boston.
- Rob chooses to accompany her; he may complain.
- Cait's Boston detour does not *cause* the lab's destruction.
- Cait offers Rob a way around the painful road; he chooses to continue.
- The machines do not target the pair personally.
- Cait proposes the forest route; Rob accepts because the ordinary road is gone.
- Cait chooses the New Hampshire detour, but the next chapter must show Rob can enjoy that choice.

### Technical / worldbuilding audit

- 2994 is the story date. Mass uploading happened about **ten years earlier**, not a thousand. The M3 is an old model, not a thousand-year-abandoned object.
- The forest detour should be depicted as a **real vehicle-access road**, not an ATV or hiking track.
- The physical lab and the BMW need a salvage/transport explanation compatible with the garage and ownership system.
- A tower collapse viewed from Blue Hills must be legible at the chosen overlook; avoid pretending every street-level camera can see all of Boston.
- The earlier Cait awakening and gas station encounter are **not** retconned or shown here.
- The chapter gives no final explanation for Heaven, the machine world's apparent mind, or the fate of Rob.

## Discussion questions for writers, reviewers, and readers

1. Why might Rob's concern on Tower Hill feel loving on a first playthrough and frightened on a second? Is either interpretation necessarily wrong?
2. Does Cait's offer to take another road undermine her independence, or demonstrate it? What on-screen action supports your answer?
3. What does Rob's care for a cracked screwdriver tell us that a speech about losing his home could not?
4. Does the machine convoy show hostility, ignorance, or a scale of purpose too large for the pair to read? Which facts can we actually see?
5. Why is a funny memory about a lover more revealing than a carefully worded confession of grief?
6. Is “She went to Heaven” fair misdirection? What knowledge would make it feel like a cheat?
7. Could the Boston demolition be a productive act from a perspective the protagonists don't possess?
8. Why does Jim's damaged car need to remain optional even if Jim becomes emotionally important in the main story?
9. What exactly changes between the first scene and the last? Is it circumstance, trust, geography, or the protagonists' understanding of one another?
10. After the chapter, is the player more curious about the machines, the reason for the ocean trip, or Rob's past? Can the script support all three without telling viewers what to think?

## Editorial pass/fail checklist

- [ ] The main story's cause-and-effect chain is perceivable without this guide.
- [ ] The original Tower Hill radio and garage beats are respected.
- [ ] The game does not unveil Heaven before St. Louis.
- [ ] Cait doesn't lose agency merely because the lab is destroyed.
- [ ] The machine convoy does not behave like a vindictive robot army.
- [ ] Rob is allowed to be funny, resourceful, angry and tender without being a wounded saint.
- [ ] Cait is allowed to argue, change course and give care without becoming a moral lecturer.
- [ ] The Providence scene earns “Again” literally before asking for a deeper reading.
- [ ] Jim's M3 has an observable reason to be saved, without announcing why it matters.
- [ ] At least one scene could be enjoyed without knowing any larger lore (preferably several).
- [ ] Every mandatory race has a distinct play function and consequence.
- [ ] The New Hampshire hook follows a voluntary scenic detour, not a conveniently chained distress signal.
- [ ] No player's route success or failure accidentally decides the reserved ending.

**Key editorial principle:** The story may be about love and loss. The characters should mostly be trying to get somewhere, keep a car running, look at a city, revisit a road, or survive a mistake. Meaning is what emerges when we remember what they did.
